Shifting Public Opinion, Unstable Power: Political Turmoil Around the World

FSFactory Magazine 팀 · 9 min read

Source: KBS 세계는지금 · 요동치는 민심 흔들리는 권력 (KBS_2026.07.25.방송). Summarised from that video.

Political upheaval, marked by fluctuating public sentiment and shaking power, continues unabated across the globe. From citizen resistance against long-term rule and corruption to pre-election terrorism, opposition to labor reforms, and an uncertain future, each nation faces its own chaotic situation.

Uganda: 40 Years of Long-Term Rule and Public Resistance

In Kampala, the capital of Uganda, located in east-central Africa, police used violence against protesting citizens. This bloodshed stemmed from the Ugandan presidential election held on January 15, 2026. President Yoweri Museveni, 81, challenged for his seventh presidential term, promising economic growth.

President Museveni, a former rebel leader, has been in power since 1986. He paved the way for nearly 40 years of rule by abolishing presidential term limits in 2005 and age restrictions in 2017. One media outlet asked President Museveni, "You have ruled for half your life. Will you rule for the rest of it?", to which he replied, "That is for the people to decide."

However, citizens, weary of this chronic corruption, began anti-government protests in the streets from 2010. Amidst growing calls for resistance, Robert Kyagulanyi Ssentamu (Bobi Wine), a rapper from the slums, ran as a new opposition candidate in this election. As a result of the election, President Museveni won with 71.65% of the votes. Following the confirmation of President Museveni's seventh consecutive term, repression against Bobi Wine began. Threatening measures followed, including the explosion of a drone used to monitor Bobi Wine and government forces raiding his home.

Bangladesh: Escalating Political Violence Ahead of the General Election

Bangladesh faces a general election on January 12. After the Election Commission announced the election date on December 11 last year, the former ruling party Awami League, to which former Prime Minister Hasina belonged, was banned from activity and disqualified from the election. The day after the election announcement, on December 12, Hadi, a key figure in the 2024 anti-Hasina student uprising and a young leader planning to run as an independent in the next general election, was shot.

Police are continuing investigations, viewing the suspects in Hadi's shooting as linked to a youth organization under former Prime Minister Hasina's Awami League, suggesting the crime was politically motivated retaliation. Tens of thousands gathered at Hadi's funeral, and the cry 'We are Hadi' spread nationwide. Hadi's death intensified public anger and anti-Hasina sentiment. Protesters also burned down two major Bangladeshi newspaper offices. Since the election announcement, at least 16 politicians have died, and political violence has surged. One Bangladeshi citizen stated, 'I am participating in an election after 17 years,' expressing hope for a fair government to emerge. The nation stands at a crossroads, where its fate hangs in the balance.

Argentina: Government-Citizen Clash Over Labor Reforms

In Argentina, a general strike on February 19 paralyzed the air transportation system. On the day of the strike, Argentine citizens gathered in front of the National Congress to protest against the Milei government's labor reforms. Protesters shouted, 'This is a nationwide general strike!', 'Down with enslaving reforms!', and 'Labor reform is over!'

Government forces suppressed the protesters with tear gas and water cannons. During this process, about 10 protesters were arrested, and some participants expressed pain. Nancy Pazos, a journalist critical of the Milei government, demonstrated her will to resist with a chain performance.

Since the Milei government implemented reforms in 2023, the number of workers has decreased by approximately 270,000 over two years. Last December, President Milei, entering his second year in office, submitted a new labor modernization bill. The bill includes provisions allowing a maximum of 12 hours of work per day and easing corporate layoff regulations. When the bill was approved by the House of Representatives on February 20, public anxiety grew further. Ultimately, on February 27, the labor reform bill passed the Senate.

Peru, Nepal, Colombia: Presidential Impeachment and Anti-Government Protests

In Peru, President José Hery, who succeeded to the presidency as Speaker of the National Congress, was impeached just four months after taking office. He had sparked controversy after being seen wearing a hood and secretly meeting someone while under suspicion of colluding with a Chinese businessman. This marked the eighth change of Peruvian president in 10 years since 2016, effectively meaning a president with a five-year term changed on average every 1 year and 4 months.

In Nepal, in September 2025, Nepalese youth staged large-scale anti-government protests, rebelling against the corruption and incompetence of established politics. The protests escalated further with casualties, including 76 demonstrators killed during harsh police crackdowns, leading to attacks on the homes of major politicians and the burning of government buildings. Ultimately, Prime Minister Sharma Oli resigned just four days into the protests, and an interim government took power. However, with delays in punishing those responsible for the crackdown and the return of former Prime Minister Oli to the general election, a sense of crisis heightened among the youth. Balen, who emerged as an alternative among young people after the protests, captivated voters with a platform centered on dismantling the old system and addressing public welfare. One Balen supporter expressed hope, saying, 'Thanks to Balen, this country is happy and tears of joy are flowing,' adding, 'Balen will build this country.'

In southwestern Colombia, a terrorist incident occurred on the 25th of last month, where explosives were dropped onto a minivan passing on a highway. In southwestern Colombia, 26 terror incidents occurred over two days, leading to increasing civilian casualties. The recent surge in terrorism ahead of the presidential election (May 31), which is one month away, appears to reflect criminal organizations' intentions to sow social unrest, disrupt the election, and expand their regional influence. Concerns are also growing regarding the safety of candidates, with three prominent presidential candidates having already publicly stated they received death threats.

Bolivia and Japan: Unstable Governments and Citizen Concerns

In La Paz, Bolivia, anti-government protests have been ongoing for a month. The spark for the protests was the 'Land Reclassification Law.' Bolivian President Rodrigo Paz, who took office as the first center-right president in 20 years, introduced a law allowing the land of indigenous people and farmers to be used as loan collateral. People in a predominantly agricultural country, believing they had lost their right to protect their land, reacted vehemently. The economic crisis brought on by President Paz's austerity policies, including the abolition of fuel subsidies, fueled anger. Protests, which began with indigenous groups, expanded to include miners and teachers. In this situation, President Paz repealed a law concerning the declaration of a state of emergency, hinting at the possibility of martial law. During the crackdown on protests, which involved the military and police, many were injured, over 120 people were arrested, and 7 died. With neither side showing signs of backing down in the blockaded city, attention is now focused on Bolivia's future.

In Japan, Prime Minister Takaichi's approval rating remained high at 61% at the time of broadcast, even as she reached her six-month mark in office. However, a professor at Hosei University pointed out that there was significant criticism that this was unrelated to actual performance. In particular, as discussions on constitutional revision were formalized based on a majority in the House of Representatives, not only expectations but also concerns are rising among young people. Tanaka Yusuke (university student) said, 'It makes me wonder what exactly 'national interest' and a 'strong nation' mean.' Ichimura Yusei (university student) stated, 'In the current urgent security environment, it is necessary to properly revise the constitution to make the Japanese archipelago strong and prosperous.' Kobayashi Chisato (university student) expressed concern, saying, 'The more the Takaichi administration uses strong, populist rhetoric, the higher the approval ratings will soar,' adding, 'This is a truly dangerous phenomenon.' The Takaichi administration faces high public expectations and concerns, commensurate with its high approval ratings, as to whether it will bring about new changes or merely repeat past political practices.

The World Now Stands at a Crossroads of Great Transition

The world now stands at a great crossroads, navigating chaos, crisis, and conflict, while seeking a new world through innovation. Public anger and the shaking of power add uncertainty to the politics of each nation, and the voices of citizens are acting as crucial variables in determining the future.
