US Intervention Selling Euros to Buy Yen Driven by Desire to Stabilize Treasury Yields

FSFactory Magazine 팀 · 4 min read

Source: KBS 세계는지금 · 미국의 엔화 구출 작전 (KBS_463회_2026.08.15.방송). Summarised from that video.

Yen Hits 40-Year Low as Japanese Small Businesses Face Wave of Bankruptcies

The yen-dollar exchange rate surpassed 163 yen per dollar, driving the value of the Japanese currency to its lowest level in 40 years. Rising crude oil prices due to a prolonged war involving Iran and the Japanese government's continued expansionary fiscal stance are cited as primary factors fueling the yen's weakness.

Considering that the fair exchange rate calculated by the "Tonkatsu Curry Index"—Japan's version of the Big Mac Index—is around 62 yen per dollar, the yen's current value is undervalued by more than 60%. Although foreign tourist spending surged due to the plunge in the yen, rising raw material import prices sharply increased cost burdens for domestic small and medium-sized enterprises. Indeed, more than 5,000 Japanese companies went bankrupt in the first half of the year alone, with small businesses employing fewer than 10 people accounting for 90% of the total.

The US's Sudden FX Intervention and Rhetoric on the US-Japan Alliance

As the super-weak yen persisted, the US government directly intervened in foreign exchange markets. During an official meeting held at the US presidential retreat Camp David, a note held by the Treasury Secretary was spotted with the line "Purchase of Japanese yen worth $5 billion to $10 billion," after which both governments launched a surprise market intervention.

US President Donald Trump spoke to reporters aboard Air Force One regarding the measure as follows:

They have a weakening yen and they wanted a little bit of help, and we're always there for Japan. Japan's been very good to us, with the exception of course of Pearl Harbor. We will say signal of friendship.

"They have a weakening yen and they wanted a little bit of help, and we're always there for Japan. Japan's been very good to us, with the exception of course of Pearl Harbor. We will say signal of friendship."

The US Treasury Secretary also confirmed the official intervention in an interview:

It's more than just a market intervention. Through our conversations with them, we believe that they are going to continue to put the right policies in place that will lead the yen to get back to more of a normal equilibrium price.

"It's more than just a market intervention. Through our conversations with them, we believe that they are going to continue to put the right policies in place that will lead the yen to get back to more of a normal equilibrium price."

The True Motive Behind Intervention: Defending Against a US Treasury Sell-Off and Mortgage Rate Spikes

Experts note that the method of US intervention this time differs from past practices. While typical exchange rate intervention involves selling one's own currency (the dollar) to buy the target currency, the US Treasury sold off its euro holdings to buy yen. Because this was conducted without prior consultation with the European Union (EU), it sparked fallout, causing the euro's value to plunge sharply.

Underlying the US decision to defend the yen even by selling euros is instability in its domestic Treasury market. If the yen plummets, the Japanese government would sell off large quantities of US Treasuries in order to defend the FX market. A flood of Treasury sales would drive down bond prices and cause Treasury yields to skyrocket.

With the US 10-year Treasury yield already rising to 4.7% and the 30-year yield reaching 5.2%, mortgage rates surged to 6.58%, placing a severe burden on the everyday American economy. Given that US national debt stands at approximately $40 trillion—generating over 4 trillion won in daily interest expenses alone—every 0.1 percentage point increase in Treasury yields adds hundreds of trillions of won in interest costs, making it imperative for the US to prevent Japan from selling US Treasuries.

FIMA Repo Proposal and Future Market Outlook

The US Treasury proposed that Japan utilize the Federal Reserve's FIMA Repo Facility (treasury-backed lending) so that Japan can secure dollars without selling US Treasuries. The calculation was to eliminate concerns over Treasury sell-offs by encouraging Japan to borrow dollars using US Treasuries as collateral to defend the yen.

However, experts point out that the effectiveness is limited due to restrictions on eligible countries and caps under the FIMA Repo Facility, as well as interest burdens exceeding the US policy rate. Major research institutions, including the Peterson Institute for International Economics (PIIE), analyzed that the impact of FX defense could be short-lived because root causes—such as Japan's excessive debt, risks from the war involving Iran, and uncertainty surrounding the Japanese government's willingness to tighten fiscal policy—remain unresolved. Experts warned that repeated market interventions could erode confidence in government policy and increase financial market uncertainty over the long term.
